How it works

As a ball grows, more pairs fit inside it. The scaling of that count gives a view of the geometry across distance scales.

C(r)=2N(N−1)∑i<j1[‖xi−xj‖≤r]

In a scaling region, C(r) behaves approximately like r raised to D₂. The slope of log C(r) against log r estimates D₂.

  1. Count distinct pairs.For each radius, find the fraction of unordered pairs separated by at most that radius. Self-pairs are excluded.
  2. Find a useful scale interval.The highlighted portion of the plot is the interval used in the fit.
  3. Read the slope.Fit a straight line in log–log space. Compare intervals before trusting a single number.

The version in this notebook

This lab fits 24 log-spaced radii between the 1st and 10th percentiles of pair distances by default. Adjust the interval in the controls to explore other scales. The reported slope is a finite-scale estimate, not the mathematical limit as the radius tends to zero.

What to watch for

Tiny radii contain too few pairs; large radii saturate. Noise and boundaries can erase a useful scaling region. A high R² alone does not establish a correct dimension.
